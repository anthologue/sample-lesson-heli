Section 01

The Rotary Wing: Generating Lift from Motion

An airplane wing requires the entire aircraft to speed forward through the air mass. A helicopter bypasses this limitation by mounting multiple wings (called rotor blades) on a vertical mast, rotating them through the air even when the fuselage is completely motionless on the ground.

Each rotor blade is shaped with an aerodynamic airfoil cross-section. When the blade slices through the air, it alters airflow velocity:

  • A. Bernoulli’s Principle: Air moves faster over the curved upper surface of the blade than below it, producing lower static pressure on top.
  • B. Newton’s Third Law: The blade's angle of attack deflects massive volumes of air downwards (induced flow or downwash). In response, the air imparts an equal upward reaction force: Lift.

Crucially, rotor blade velocity is not uniform. The blade tip travels along a vastly larger circumference than the blade root for every revolution ($V = \omega \cdot r$). Consequently, blade tips encounter very high relative wind speed, necessitating blade twist (built-in wash-out) to equalize lift distribution across the blade length.

Section 02

The Three Primary Flight Controls & The Swashplate

A helicopter pilot cannot simply turn a steering wheel. Flight requires coordinating three distinct control inputs, orchestrated mechanically through one of the most ingenious engineering marvels ever created: the Swashplate Assembly.

01

Collective Pitch Lever

Mounted to the pilot's left side. Pulling up moves the entire swashplate assembly vertically up the mast, increasing the angle of attack of all blades simultaneously by the same amount. Result: total rotor thrust increases, and the helicopter climbs.

Axis: Vertical (Climb / Sink)
02

Cyclic Stick

Held in the right hand between the pilot's knees. Tilting this stick tilts the swashplate. Blade pitch now varies continuously as each blade orbits around the rotor disc—climbing on one side and dipping on the opposite. The rotor disc tilts, directing thrust forward, back, left, or right.

Axis: Pitch & Roll (Translation)
03

Anti-Torque Pedals

Foot pedals operate pitch links on the tail rotor. Pushing the left or right pedal varies the horizontal thrust at the tail, overcoming main rotor engine torque to point the nose or execute pirouette yaw maneuvers in hover.

Axis: Yaw (Heading Control)

The Dual-Ring Swashplate Mechanism Explained

How do stationary pilot controls communicate with spinning rotor blades spinning at 400 RPM?

The swashplate consists of two concentric rings separated by a giant precision bearing:

  • 1. Stationary Ring (Bottom): Connected via mechanical pushrods to the cyclic and collective controls. Does NOT rotate, but can slide vertically and tilt in any direction.
  • 2. Rotating Ring (Top): Locked to the rotor mast via scissors linkages and spins synchronously with the blades. Pitch control rods link this ring directly to each blade horn.

When the pilot tilts the stationary plate, the rotating plate follows its exact orientation. As each blade sweeps across the high side of the tilt, its pitch horn twists the blade to high pitch; when it sweeps to the low side, its pitch drops.

Section 03

Newton’s Third Law & The Anti-Torque Problem

Whenever an internal combustion engine or turboshaft spins the main rotor blades clockwise (viewed from above), Newton's Third Law (Action and Reaction) applies instantly: the fuselage experiences an equal and opposite torque seeking to spin counter-clockwise.

TORQUE REACTION EQUATION $$Q = \frac{P}{\Omega} \implies F_{\text{tail}} \times d_{\text{tail\_arm}} = Q_{\text{engine}}$$

To stop the cabin from spinning out of control, the tail rotor must generate sideways thrust equal to the engine torque divided by the tail boom length.

Because main rotor torque increases exponentially whenever the pilot pulls collective to generate more lift, the pilot (or automatic yaw governor) must continuously add anti-torque pedal pressure.

Common Anti-Torque Solutions

1. Conventional Tail Rotor Bell 206, UH-60

Open 2 or 4-bladed auxiliary vertical rotor mounted at the tail. Highly efficient, but vulnerable to obstacle strikes.

2. Fenestron (Fan-in-fin) Airbus H135/H145

Shrouded multi-blade ducted fan built into the vertical fin. Safer for ground personnel and quieter, though slightly heavier.

3. NOTAR (No Tail Rotor) MD 520N

Uses a low-pressure air blower and the Coandă effect along the tail boom plus a directable jet thruster at the end.

4. Coaxial / Tandem Rotors Kamov Ka-52, CH-47 Chinook

Two rotors spinning in opposite directions completely cancel each other’s torque. 100% of engine power goes into lift.

Section 04

Complex Rotor Dynamics: Flapping & Precession

Forward Flight Instability

Dissymmetry of Lift

In a hover, both blades encounter identical airspeed ($\omega \cdot r$). But as the helicopter flies forward at speed $V_\infty$:

Advancing Blade: Airspeed = $V_{\text{rot}} + V_{\infty}$
Generates huge lift spike!
Retreating Blade: Airspeed = $V_{\text{rot}} - V_{\infty}$
Suffers lift drop & stall!

Without correction, the helicopter would violently roll over toward the retreating blade side. Juan de la Cierva solved this by introducing the Flapping Hinge. The advancing blade flaps upward (reducing its effective angle of attack), while the retreating blade flaps downward (increasing angle of attack), self-balancing the lift.

90° Phase Delay

Gyroscopic Precession

A spinning rotor disc behaves precisely like a giant gyroscope. When a force is applied to tilt a spinning gyro, the resultant movement occurs not where the force was exerted, but 90 degrees later in the direction of rotation.

THE PILOT'S TRICK: Phase Angle = 90°

To tilt the rotor disc forward, the cyclic pitch must change maximum blade angle over the left or right side of the helicopter, so the peak flapping reaction reaches maximum downward deflection exactly at the nose 90° later. The swashplate pitch links are mechanically offset by 90° to do this automatically!

This elegant harmony between fluid dynamics and rigid-body mechanics makes controllable flight possible.

Section 05

Special Flight Regimes: Ground Effect & Autorotation

IGE

Ground Effect (IGE vs OGE)

When hovering within one rotor diameter of the terrain, the solid ground physically blocks the downward airflow, causing it to curl outward. This substantially reduces blade tip vortex formation and induced drag, enabling the helicopter to hover with 15% to 25% less engine power than in Out of Ground Effect (OGE).

VRS

Vortex Ring State

If a helicopter descends too quickly at near-zero forward airspeed, it sinks into its own violent downwash. The air recirculates upward through the disc and curls back down in a churning donut vortex. Applying more collective pitch merely accelerates the vortex, increasing the descent rate dangerously ("settling with power").

AUTO

Autorotation: Engine Off Landing

If the engine fails, a helicopter does not fall like a stone. The pilot immediately drops collective pitch to minimum and uncouples the engine via a freewheeling clutch. Upward air through the descending rotor spins the blades like a windmill. Right before touchdown, the pilot raises collective, using the stored rotor momentum to cushion a gentle touchdown.
